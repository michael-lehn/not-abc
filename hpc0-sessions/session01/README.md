# Lecturer Notes — Worksheet 1

This repository contains the lecturer notes for **Worksheet 1** of the HPC0 course.

## Prerequisites

Before students start working on the worksheet in the classroom, they are
expected to watch the following videos:

- https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UW_aVHYz3Y0
- https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gnplpV9H5Ko
- https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ycfB_F1K260

At Ulm University, these videos are also made available through Moodle for
offline viewing. The accompanying lecture slides are distributed as well and
are included in this repository.

The videos contain live programming sessions. Students are expected to type the
programs themselves while watching the videos rather than simply watching
passively. Programming is a practical skill that can only be learned by
actually writing code. Simply watching someone else program is about as
effective as learning mathematics by only watching someone else solve
exercises.

Of course, every instructor develops their own strategies for encouraging
active participation. In our experience, this becomes much easier once you get
to know your students.

## Classroom Session

The worksheet is completed during a two-hour classroom session. Students work
on their own laptops while the instructors circulate through the room, answer
questions, and provide individual support whenever necessary.

Although the worksheet builds directly on the material presented in the videos,
it also introduces ideas whose significance only becomes apparent later in the
course. Among other things, students encounter

- lexical analysis (and finite-state machines),
- parsing,
- code generation,
- the Unix shell,
- text editors,
- additional language features.

The long-term structure of the course is intentionally not explained in detail
at this point. Rather than spending a significant amount of time motivating
future topics, we prefer students to start programming as early as possible.

The worksheet therefore focuses on practical skills such as

- editing source code,
- reading compiler error messages,
- working with the terminal,
- experimenting with programs, and
- following the edit → compile → run workflow.

Only much later in the course—after students have built a simple ALU with
registers and implemented instructions such as `load`, `add`, and `mul`—do many
of them realize that they had already written a small compiler during the very
first worksheet. That delayed realization is intentional and often leads to a
memorable "aha!" moment.
