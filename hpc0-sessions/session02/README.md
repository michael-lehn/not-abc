# Session 02 — Logic Gates

This directory contains the lecturer notes accompanying **Worksheet 2** of the
HPC0 course.

The session combines mathematical reasoning, digital circuit simulation, and
real hardware experiments. While the worksheet itself is largely self-contained,
students are expected to watch the following videos beforehand.

## Preparation

Students should watch these videos before coming to class.

- [Getting Started with Logic Gates](https://youtu.be/8REMztSzANA)
- [Numbers and Numerals: Representing Unsigned Integers with Bits](https://youtu.be/EH_nPckfK1k)
- [Using Logic Gates to Add Integers](https://youtu.be/OFFAKWHoja8)

These videos were recorded several years ago. Towards the end of some of them,
the audio quality deteriorates slightly. Re-recording them has simply never
made it to the top of my priority list.

Fortunately, students usually forgive these imperfections. Session 2 is one of
the most interactive sessions of the entire course, and the experiments tend to
leave a much stronger impression than the videos themselves.

---

## Hardware Demonstrations

Whenever possible, I accompany this session with real hardware.

I usually bring

- laboratory power supplies,
- oscilloscopes,
- breadboards,
- discrete NPN transistors,
- LEDs,
- logic ICs,
- and various electronic components.

The goal is simple:

> Everything students simulate in CircuitVerse can also be observed in a real
> electronic circuit.

For example, after discussing propagation delays, students can immediately see
the same effect on an oscilloscope. Likewise, NAND gates are not only simulated
but also built from individual transistors.

This helps students realize that digital logic is not an abstract mathematical
game but an engineering discipline built on physical devices.

---

## Beyond the Videos

The worksheet intentionally goes beyond the material covered in the preparation
videos.

The first half reinforces combinational logic through increasingly sophisticated
examples.

The later steps already provide an experimental introduction to **sequential
logic**. Students encounter D latches and D flip-flops long before the formal
lecture on the topic. Experience has shown that seeing these concepts once
before greatly reduces the cognitive load when they appear again in later
sessions.

## Teaching Assistants

I am usually supported by former participants from the
[Schülerforschungszentrum Ulm](https://sfz-bw.de/ulm-sfz-schuelerforschungszentrum-suedwuerttemberg/).

Despite their age (many are only 13–16 years old), they are remarkably
experienced. Most have already built computers from logic gates, implemented
processors on FPGAs, or programmed embedded systems.

One unexpected benefit is psychological rather than technical.

University students quickly stop worrying that the material might be "too hard"
when a fourteen-year-old calmly explains how to build a NAND gate from two
transistors or demonstrates propagation delays with an oscilloscope.

Their presence also helps enormously during the first part of the session,
where installation issues from Session 0 or Session 1 occasionally still need
to be resolved while the rest of the class has already started working.

I should probably also mention that they are volunteers. They are not paid for
their work. In fact, convincing them to come is usually not the difficult part.
More often, they have to convince their parents to let them spend an afternoon
at the university explaining digital logic to undergraduate students.
